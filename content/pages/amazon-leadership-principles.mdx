This page is a retrieval guide, not a claim that every example has equal strength. It keeps measured outcomes, negative results, team ownership, and evidence limits explicit.

## Customer Obsession

Customer Obsession means starting from the person who must use the result, then working backward instead of treating implementation as the finish line. In my experience, the closest equivalent is serving learners, collaborators, and maintainers: publishing NoteNextra before the larger platform was complete, responding to a reader-reported mathematical error, and building SophiasPath around what a teammate needed to publish rather than around the backend I happened to want to build.

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>NoteNextra / Notechondria</summary>

**Situation:** Hundreds of course notes could help learners, but a larger publishing system was not stable enough.

**Task:** Publish a useful, searchable archive without waiting for the larger platform.

**Action:** Built a static site with 551 Markdown/MDX documents, weekly Algolia indexing, and correction paths.

**Result:** The archive serves about 10 weekly users; a reader-reported Math 4121 error was corrected and credited.

[Read the project](/projects/notenextra-notechondria/)

</details>

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>SophiasPath</summary>

**Situation:** A teammate needed to publish structured philosophy research connecting thinkers, writings, and concepts.

**Task:** Own the backend, data model, deployment, and coordination while supporting frontend integration.

**Action:** Built Django/PostgreSQL models, APIs, Markdown import, administration, Docker/Nginx deployment, and integration fixes.

**Result:** The team publicly deployed a usable site; no reliable audience analytics were retained.

[Read the project](/projects/sophiaspath/)

</details>

## Ownership

Ownership means accepting responsibility for the outcome beyond the narrow boundary of an assigned role. In the MTA Strategy Optimizer, I did not limit myself to the data-science assignment I entered with: I initiated conversations across departments, worked closely with algorithm teams, and analyzed the data model required by each layer so the overall system could integrate. In Chinese Groundbreakers, I similarly continued proposing events, maintaining resources, and helping students when senior leadership had less capacity.

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>MTA Strategy Optimizer</summary>

**Situation:** A 25-person project lacked shared data contracts while approved source data was unavailable.

**Task:** Create dependable upstream data and common interfaces without formal authority, extending beyond the data-science responsibilities originally assigned to me.

**Action:** I delivered a fixed-seed baseline in two days, then actively consulted contributors outside the data-science department—especially algorithm teams—to understand the data model, inputs, outputs, and evaluation concerns at each layer. I translated those requirements into typed schemas, validation, manifests, adapters, tests, and documentation.

**Result:** The active subgroup integrated around the shared interfaces; the mentor called this the most mature implementation and best match for expectations.

[Read the project](/projects/mta-strategy-optimizer/)

</details>

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>Chinese Groundbreakers</summary>

**Situation:** Club leaders had reduced capacity during their final semesters, and activity was declining.

**Task:** Keep the club useful and responsive beyond assigned website and media duties.

**Action:** Proposed timely events, maintained content, sought feedback, attended nearly every event, and offered personalized help.

**Result:** The club continued serving more than 300 members, and course support helped 15 students plan schedules.

[Read the project](/projects/washington-university-chinese-groundbreakers/)

</details>

## Are Right, A Lot

I do not treat “right” as a fixed position owned by one person. People act under different objectives, constraints, risk tolerances, and evaluation metrics, so a decision that looks inappropriate under one objective may be coherent under another. For me, this principle means actively seeking those perspectives, identifying what each person is optimizing, and comparing alternatives across measures such as cost, reliability, learning value, and implementation risk before committing to a decision.

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>ATTPDRL</summary>

**Situation:** A trailer-truck parking task allowed several plausible research paths, but nonlinear dynamics, sparse success, and expensive exploration made every choice a trade-off.

**Task:** Explore trajectory-free deep reinforcement learning without assuming that the cheapest, fastest, or most reliable implementation was automatically the only correct direction.

**Action:** The team investigated PPO, DDPG, and DQN paths, built supporting utilities, adapters, and visualizations, and compared outcomes through multiple metrics including convergence, implementation reliability, computational cost, and the knowledge gained from unsuccessful trials.

**Result:** Straight driving converged while 90-degree steering did not after five million steps. Rather than forcing one definition of success, we used the contrast between paths and metrics to revise the scope and clarify what each approach could establish.

[Read the project](/projects/attpdrl/)

</details>

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>MTA Strategy Optimizer</summary>

**Situation:** Data-science, algorithm, dashboard, and other project groups were developing different layers with different assumptions about the data they needed and the outcomes they were optimizing.

**Task:** Understand those perspectives well enough to design shared interfaces, even though cross-department integration and analysis of every layer's data model were outside my original AI Engineer responsibilities.

**Action:** I actively spoke with contributors beyond the data-science group, worked closely with algorithm teams, analyzed the input and output model required at each layer, and translated competing implementation concerns into shared schemas and extension points.

**Result:** The active subgroup integrated around a common contract, while the discussions exposed why teams made different choices under different metrics rather than reducing disagreement to one side being right.

[Read the project](/projects/mta-strategy-optimizer/)

</details>

## Learn and Be Curious

Learn and Be Curious describes the habit of following a question beyond the knowledge already required by a role or course. My mathematical research pushed me across probability, geometry, and quantum information, while projects such as Mon3tr forced me to connect diffusion models with 3D reconstruction. I tend to learn by building enough of the surrounding system to discover where my understanding actually breaks.

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>Mathematical Research</summary>

**Situation:** A research path required connecting high-dimensional probability, geometry, and quantum information.

**Task:** Understand the chain behind typical entanglement rather than treat the result as a black box.

**Action:** Studied the necessary fields and presented intermediate and thesis work.

**Result:** Completed the year-long path and presented the senior honors thesis on March 26, 2026.

</details>

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>Mon3tr</summary>

**Situation:** A project tested whether plausible 2D inpainting could fill missing views in 3D reconstruction.

**Task:** Integrate reconstruction and diffusion into a runnable iterative pipeline.

**Action:** Built the pipeline and UI, then inspected intermediate views and resulting geometry.

**Result:** The code path ran, but geometry was unusable; the negative result exposed multi-view inconsistency.

[Read the project](/projects/mon3tr/)

</details>

My self-hosted platform relies on open-source and portable components, circtime experiments with backend-free scheduled monitoring, and several public sites use static deployment where a permanent server would add little value. Constraints often force me to make the system simpler and more transferable.

In my projects, that means crediting reader corrections, documenting negative experimental results, preserving confidentiality boundaries, and distinguishing a working prototype from a validated production system. ## Dive Deep

Dive Deep means tracing an outcome through the underlying data, implementation, and assumptions rather than stopping at a dashboard or summary metric. The reliability case study required separating weekday demand, station history, peer behavior, and factor enrichment; my infrastructure work required understanding routing, storage, migration, and recovery together. I am most useful when I can move between the system-level picture and the exact mechanism producing it.

<details className="principle-example">
<summary>Reliability Event Detection</summary>

**Situation:** Operational logs and metadata had no labeled failures, obscuring isolated versus correlated problems.

**Task:** Build a reproducible daily detector and communicate auditable evidence without exposing private data.

**Action:** Used weekday baselines, leave-one-out peers, tail tests, effect-size thresholds, enrichment, diagnostics, and drill-down views.

**Result:** The notebook produced the required daily reporting interface and event-level diagnostics while preserving disclosure boundaries.

I try to make disagreement specific and testable rather than personal.

## Deliver Results

Deliver Results means producing the outcome that matters under the actual constraints, not merely completing activity that resembles progress. My strongest examples have concrete endpoints: a deployed archive, a playable game, an integrated data contract, a daily reliability report, or a migration that can be repeated in about 30 minutes. I also distinguish delivery from success when a pipeline runs but the scientific or operational result is still inadequate.
